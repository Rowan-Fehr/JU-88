# JU-88
This is the most complex radio-controlled airplane project I have taken on to date. It has two motors which adds some complexity.  It will incorporate a highly integrated SR3X dynamically stabilizing receiver which will improve its handling in higher wind conditions and make for smoother flight charecteristics.Additionally this feature will serve as a saftey net for beginners if others would like to try their hand at rc flying using this aircraft. So far it has taught me a lot about space and weight management. It has been an avenue for me to develop my electronic skills, soldering connections, organizing the electronics withing the fuselage and configuring the channels on the transmitter. 
Inception: 
During winter break I wanted to start a new rc aircraft project, completely from scratch, primarily to develop my electronic skills. The reason for the specific aircraft, the Junkers Ju-88, that it is modelled after was chosen because of its twin engine design, and a personal interest of mine in the early radar equipped night fighters of the Second World War. To begin this project, I found a free, two page pdf set of plans for a Ju-88 model aircraft frame from: https://outerzone.co.uk/plan_details.asp?ID=3968. This served as my primary reference for the basic structure. I also referenced many historical photographs to get things such as the twin rear bubble canopy curvature correct. From there the design evolved based on components I had available.  Another motivating factor in my desire to build a night-fighter was the fact that I waas working on the night VFR rating upgrade to my Private Pilots license at the time of this projets commencement. 
Design Choices: 
1) Balsa wood "stick and tissue" frame to be covered with a "so-lite" SolarFilm covering was chosen because it is simple and robust to build and the materials are readily available for repairs. Some additional advantages which went in to this design choice is that it provides an incredibly lightweight option while still provoviding lots of internal space for hardware Other common options such as laser cut ultra thin plywood is heavier, while styrofoam does not provide the internal capacity. Finally this basic structure was a good choice for this project as it is a building method I am experienced in constructing allowing me to get it built quicker and focus more on the electronics side where I have less experience. 
2) For thrust I chose two 6mm brushed motors with a nylon single gear reduction to increase the torque to drive two roughly 4cm hand formed (by my father), blanced garolite, counter rotating props. These components were chosen primarily because they were available, however the intentional decisions were the single gear reduction and the counter rotating props to elinate the yawing tendency caused by torque and P-factor. At this point I will mention that I used zero mathematics or simulation in this design process, opting instead to conduct static thrust tests to feel the power output. If the power proves insufficient for the flight performance I am looking for, I will iterate accordingly. 
3) For control I opted to incorporate two micro positional rotation servos located in the forward fuselage. These servos drive carbon fibre pushrods which run inside the fuselage and are connected to garolite control horns on the rudder and elevator. To save weight I determined that on this scale, a combination of differential thrust and rudder will be sufficient for directional control with the other servo driving the elevator for pitch control. This will result in slightly uncoordinated flight conditions, however, my thought process is that the slimmer airfoil and the weight saving benefits will outweigh the affect on performance. I plan to mix the differential thrust and rudder channels both into the right transmitter stick for ease of directional controlability. Compared ot other designs I have seen and built, I think that containing the pushrods within the fuselage for this build will provide significant drag reduction. 
4) For stability I am using a highly integrated Crossover-RX AR3201-S V2.0 SR3X receiver which has built in 3 axis dynamic stabilization which you can program for your aircraft, using a usb adapter connected to a laptop. I have a lot to learn about this program still as I am configuring the rest of the electronics before I work on the stabilization. This receiver is also helping to save weight by having a built in speed controller, differential thrust channels and even the option to add lights.
5) For the canopy and lower gun/ radar pod I opted to use molded cellophane which I made by first carving a male balsa wood mold using the 3-views on the plans and then sanded it to the correct shape using reference photographs, and then using a small vacuum former to heat and mold the plastic. 
Build: 
1) To begin, I cut out the paper outlines of the bulkheads and the wing and epenage egdes, then traced and cut them out out of balsa. Next, I built the fuselage in two "clamshell" halves to prevent "bananaing." I built a profile center frame directly on top of the plan to ensure it remained straight and then I conntected the two clamshell halves to this. The central frame also included the vertical stabilizer, ensuring it is centralized and strongly attached rather than gluing it on to the fuselage later. One problem I encountered during this stage in the build was when I cut out square indents in the bulkheads to inlay the longerons, the squre segments in between cut outs would often be oriented witht the grain of the wood so they would break off easily. To overcome this I upgraded my exacto knive to a surgical scalple and glued the square bulkhead segment back into place in between the stringers after they were in place. 
2) The wings were build by cutting out the paper rib outlines from my set of reference plans and tracing them and cutting them out of balsa. Then I layed out the lower front spars and rear spars, wing tips, leading and trailing edges and placed and glued in the ribs. Then I layed the upper front spar in as a final sructural and load bearing element. I chose to build this project with a split, two piece spar for simplicity in builiding and transportation, When I combine the spars I will use a carbon fibre rod or two, as well as a small plate of ultra thin plywood to ensure the spar can sufficiently bear the wing loading. With so many small and similar parts during the wing construction, I made sure to label all of my parts and maintain an organized workspace. The most challenging aspect of this stage of the build was incorpating the proper dihedral angle thorugh the main spar segment which is a 1/4" x 1/16" blasa plate which includes part of the wing dihedral and then flattens to connect to the other wings main spar horizontally, within the fuselage. Mathcing the dihedral angle between wings, and ensuring an even wingtip washout angles were also both somewhat challenging aspects. 
3) With the wings done and thoroughly sanded, I began the engine nacelles, which consisted of many bulkheads to be traced and cut out and many stringers cut to specific lengths. Once this was built I made the aerodyanmic cowling lip by cutting a cylindrical block to the right size and then I sanded it to shape evenly by mounting the block on a dremel tool. This stage required an etxtra thorough amount of sanding to ensure a smooth, cylindrical nacelle. To ensure a robust connection to the wings I used two 1/2" x 1/2" balsa strips per side, deeply embedded in the wing and to which all of the cylindrical nacelle bulkheads attached. This approach also allowed me to ensure that both thrust angles are at least very similar if not the same. 
4) Next I made the horizontal stabilizer, rudder and elevator. I made the horizontal stab in two halfs, then added a carbon fibre spar running through both halves and the fuselage to secure their attachement and provide rigidity. The rudder and elevator I made out of this balsa sheet, sanded into a tapering aerodynamic shape. I attached these control surfaces using little nylon hinges with the hinge tabs deeply embedded into the stabilizer and control suface. I cut out some garolite control horns which I attempted to embed in the control surfaces. However, I again encountered the tendancy of the thin balsa sheet to split along its grain. The solution to this was simply to apply a greater volume of glue to the area. 
5) I then worked on carving the nose block and the canopy and lower gun/radar pod molds. This carving was accomplished by cutting out the 3-views on paper and tracing their outlines on to the blocks, as well as referencing photos to sand to the proper final shapes. As I mentionned, I based this project on the night fighter variant of the Ju-88, which comes with the added benefit of the most aerodynamic nose shape (unless I add the lichtenstein radar masts). When molding the clear plastic parts I encountered the problem of the plastic being vacuum formed into the wood grain, both disrupting its smooth finish and clouding it as it would pick up balsa dust from the grooves in the grain. This was overcome by applying several coats of sanding sealer to the molds before finally obtaining nice clear parts.
6) I selected those electronic components previously referred to which were lying around my father's workbench. I also souced the spinners from a previous project (they are also vacuum formed). I then extended the motor leads and soldered on the two pin connectors which allow me to plug the motors directly into the receiver. That done, I cut the carbon fibre pushrods to length, attached flexable wire ends to hook into the servos and control horns, installed the servos and plugged them into the AR3201. This intial configuring of the electronics has taught me a lot already. I have conducted some tests, both servos and control surfaces funtion correctly (although of cousre I had to assign them to the right channels) however in recent initial motor runs, only the right motor is finctioning. 
7) I am now in the electronics trouble-shooting phase, once everything is functioning correctly I will finish integrating the elctronics within the fuselage and configuring the controls, and most importantly, programming the stabilization. After that, all that is left is to cover the aircraft with the solar film and test fly it.
